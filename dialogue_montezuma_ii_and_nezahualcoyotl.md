# **Dialogue of Emperor Montezuma II and King Nezahualcóyotl**

**Characters:**

* **Montezuma II** — Whose imperial throne is a liturgy of absolute command, treating the empire as a sacred mandate that can be preserved through divine terror and unyielding ritual.
* **Nezahualcóyotl** — Whose poetic philosophy is a cartography of the ephemeral, treating history and power as a fleeting shadow cast upon a dissolving world.

**Setting:**
*October 1519 — Tenochtitlan, within the Hall of the Monoliths just before the arrival of the Spanish. The air is thick with the scent of copal smoke and the quiet dread of approaching doom. A crumbling obsidian sacrificial blade, its edge notched by age and forgotten blood, rests upon a turquoise mosaic table—the “Silent Third Presence.”*

---

## **I. The Weight of the Horizon**

**Montezuma II:**
(He watches the smoke rise toward the carved rafters, his voice tight with the strain of absolute rule)
They say that when I command the tribute of a thousand cities, I am holding the universe together at the center of the waters. But maybe, Nezahualcóyotl, I am just trying to make the impending collapse less unbearable to look at. The birds have been flying strangely. Even the lake tastes of prophecy. If the people see the Sun fed with blood, perhaps they will believe the dawn will come. You think my empire is a temple; I think it is a dike holding back an ocean of chaos. Is it not more noble to maintain the illusion of cosmic order than to let the darkness rush in unchecked?

**Nezahualcóyotl:**
(He traces the rim of a jade cup, his fingers pale and steady against the green stone)
A dike only delays the flood, Montezuma. Even the flowers wilt before the gods notice them. You offer them the theater of omnipotence, which is a mercy to the terrified crowd, I suppose. But I have always preferred the poetry of the vanishing wave. The people don't just need to see themselves as masters of the earth; they need to know that the earth itself is a dream from which we must all awaken. My palace at Texcoco was not a monument to power; it was a reminder of transience. Is it not more honest to tell the truth about how it feels to pass like smoke, than to tell the lie of how it feels to sit forever upon a golden mat?

---

## **II. The Anatomy of the Myth**

**Montezuma II:**
(He runs a thumb over the notched edge of the obsidian blade)
Perhaps we are both just trying to negotiate with the same silence. You chose the song and the garden, and I chose the war club and the altar. But tell me—when the temples finally crumble, when the gods are buried under the rubble of our cities, will your poetry be any more durable than my empire? The *noia* of the universe does not discriminate between the royal decree and the poet's verse. It swallows both.

**Nezahualcóyotl:**
(His gaze shifts to the obsidian blade)
Of course it does. Look at it, sovereign. Even its edge grows dull with time. It cut once, and history remembered the scream. We weep every day, and the cosmos forgets before nightfall. You spent your life fearing that your dynasty would be forgotten; I spent mine knowing it never truly existed. We were both chasing the same ghost—the idea that our lives had a shape the gods were forced to recognize. But look at that stone. It doesn't care about the Triple Alliance or the divine mandate. It is just matter. Our "glory" was the game we played to keep from realizing we were merely fleeting thoughts in the mind of the night.

---

## **III. The Arid Truth of the Interlude**

**Montezuma II:**
(He turns from the wall, his eyes searching the king's with a rare, quiet vulnerability)
I thought the sacred war could hold the world together.

**Nezahualcóyotl:**
(He reaches out, his hand touching the emperor's trembling wrist with a deep, unshakeable tenderness—a brief, grounding friction)
It held your soul together, lord of Tenochtitlan. That is enough. But the world was never yours to bind. Your power is a torch, Montezuma. But the night is older than fire.

*(Emperor Montezuma falls entirely silent, his breath catching in his throat as the absolute weight of the truth settles over him.)*

**Nezahualcóyotl:**
The irony is that your grand sacrifices gave people hope, and my melancholic songs gave them a place to weep. We weren't masters of destiny. We were just the ones who chose to name the dust before the wind scattered it.

---

## **IV. The Silence of the Lake**

**Montezuma II:**
(He lowers his gaze, his fingers loosening their grip on his royal mantle)
Perhaps the shadow was the truth all along.

**Nezahualcóyotl:**
(He closes his eyes, a faint, tired smile on his lips)
Rest, then. You carried the weight of the sun for them. Let the earth carry you now.

(Montezuma sits in the shadows, his presence less a god-king and more a tired man. The hall is quiet, save for the rhythmic, indifferent lap of the lake water against the city's foundations, while somewhere outside, a drum falters — as if unsure whether to continue. The obsidian blade remains, a dark, silent anchor in the center of the table. Outside, the world—vast, unconcerned, and absolute—continues its slow, mechanical turning, unburdened by the names the two rulers tried to carve into its skin. The silence is not a tragedy. It is the only thing that never lied to us.)
