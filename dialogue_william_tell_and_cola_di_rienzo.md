# **Dialogue of William Tell and Cola di Rienzo on the Steps of a Ruined Temple**

**Characters:**

* **William Tell** — A silent, weather-beaten Swiss archer, wearing a rough woolen tunic smelling of pine smoke and mud, carrying only a stout crossbow.
* **Cola di Rienzo** — The self-proclaimed Tribune of Rome, draped in a faded, theatrical purple robe with a tarnished golden crown resting askew upon his brow, his hands still gesturing grandly to an empty square.

**Setting:**
*October 8th, 1354 — The Capitoline Hill in Rome, bathed in the sickly amber light of a dying afternoon. Surrounding them are the broken marble columns and shattered entablatures of an ancient temple, half-swallowed by weeds. A weathered, headless marble statue of a Roman emperor—the “Silent Third Presence”—lies face down in the dirt, completely indifferent to the weeds growing through its neck and the men standing over it.*

---

## **I. The Triumph of the Roman Name**

**Cola di Rienzo:**
*(Throwing his arms wide, his voice ringing with hollow grandiloquence across the empty ruins)*
Behold the center of the world, Master Tell! Here, upon these very stones, I broke the tyrannical necks of the Colonna and the Orsini. I resurrected the Roman Republic from its ashes, summoned the ambassadors of the nations, and wore the crown of the Tribune of the Holy Roman People! While you hid in your narrow Swiss valleys, shooting apples and drowning bailiffs in secret mountain passes, I filled the streets of Rome with choruses, banners, and the thunderous glory of ancient liberty! Did you feel the earth shake when the Eternal City awoke to reclaim its destiny?

**William Tell:**
*(Leaning quietly against a broken column, testing the tension of his bowstring)*
I felt only the cold rain falling on my hay-loft, Tribune. I know nothing of Rome, and I care less for your banners. When I shot Gessler, there was no choir singing in the hollow way. There was only the sound of blood dripping into the mud and the frantic gasping of my own lungs. You speak of glory as if it were a cloak to keep out the winter, but glory is just a loud noise made by men who cannot bear to look at their own graves.

---

## **II. The Theater of Power**

**Cola di Rienzo:**
*(Scowling, stepping down from his makeshift pedestal with a rustle of heavy velvet)*
You speak like a peasant who has never looked upon the face of history! You reduce the sacred fire of civic duty to mere panic in a muddy lane. Look at these stones around us—these are not just rocks; they are the physical embodiment of an eternal idea. When I stood before the people and invoked the Lex Regia, when I reminded them of the Senate and the Quirites, I was knitting them back into the divine tapestry of civilization.

Man is not a mere animal that eats and sleeps in the dirt; he is an architect of destiny. The laws of Rome, the decrees of the Republic, the memory of our consuls and triumphs—these are the celestial machinery that elevates human suffering into something sublime. We give our lives form and majesty precisely so that we are *not* just beasts whimpering in the dark.

**William Tell:**
*(Staring flatly at the tarnished crown on Cola's head)*
And yet, Tribune, your grand tapestry unraveled the moment the bread ran short and the noble houses offered bribes. Your sacred laws did not stop the mob from howling for your blood outside these very doors. You dressed the starving crowd in the ghost-clothes of dead emperors, but their bellies remained just as empty, and their fear remained just as sharp. You traded reality for theater, and you called it civilization.
*[A sudden, dry gust of wind sweeps across the Capitoline hill, lifting a cloud of ancient dust and rustling the dry weeds around the headless statue; the stone remains entirely still.]*

---

## **III. The Arid Truth**

**Cola di Rienzo:**
*(His hand trembling as it touches his crown, his voice dropping from theatrical thunder to a bitter rasp)*
They betrayed me... the very people for whom I resurrected the dream of Rome. They tore my robes and spat upon my name.

**William Tell:**
Listen to the silence of these ruins, Cola. It is *la natura matrigna*—the stepmother who watches kings and tribunes strut across her dirt, and cares no more for your Roman purple than she does for the moss on a peasant's wall. She offers the same decay to the emperor and the beggar. All creatures share *l’infelicità comune*, and the man who dies crowned in a theater of cheering crowds is just as cold, just as broken by time, and just as forgotten by the stars as the serf who dies unnoticed in a ditch.

Your "Beautiful Error" was believing that the universe keeps a ledger for human glory. The "Arid Truth" is that political revolutions are merely brief, noisy distractions from the quiet misery of existence. There is no triumph waiting at the end of history, only *il nulla*—the great nothingness—and *il gelo*, the chill of realizing that nature does not acknowledge our crowns, our speeches, or our empires.

Whether you pile up granite into the vast monuments of an Eternal City or stack rough pine logs into a lonely cabin in an alpine pass, the earth swallows both with equal indifference. The Roman Forum and the Swiss snowfield are merely different shapes of the same barren stage. The wind howls just as hollowly through a shattered triumphal arch as it does through an empty mountain gorge, and neither place remembers the names of those who wept beneath them. We invent grand words like *republic* and *glory* simply to drown out the sound of our own footsteps marching toward the dust.

**Cola di Rienzo:**
*(Staring down at his shaking hands, the grandeur entirely draining from his posture)*
Then... the eloquence? The restoration of the Senate? The centuries of longing for the Roman name? It was all for *il nulla*? A shadow playing upon a crumbling wall?

**William Tell:**
*(A pause — his voice softening into a weary, heavy flatten)*
Tribune… I, too, once thought that killing a tyrant would change the rhythm of the world. When the Swiss cantons swore their oath on the Rütli, I believed we were building something that would outlast the mountains.
*(He looks up at the pale sky.)*
But the world is not made of the greatness we invent. I mourn those illusions—they were the fire that warmed our hands in the dark. Yet mourning does not make the spring return.

---

## **IV. The Final Irony**

**Cola di Rienzo:**
*(Letting out a dry, mirthless laugh as he removes the tarnished crown and lets it drop into the weeds)*
I see now that I spent my life writing an epic tragedy upon the water. A magnificent theater of masks and crowns, built entirely to drown out the quiet voice of our common wretchedness.
*(He kicks the crown aside with a dull metallic clatter.)*
It is a *bel bell’inganno*—a very beautiful error—to imagine that men rise up for the glory of Rome, when in truth they only scream for a master who tells them their suffering has a name.

**William Tell:**
*(Turning away and adjusting his crossbow over his shoulder, his movements slow and mechanical)*
It is an error that fills the public squares for a season, Tribune. But the sun is sinking behind the hills, and the Rome you tried to wake is sleeping just as soundly as it ever did.
*(He begins to walk down the ruined slope toward the dark shadows of the city.)*
Go back to your dreams of Caesar. The mountains do not care for your republic, and the earth will take your bones just as easily as it took mine.

*(Tell disappears into the gathering gloom with the steady, unhurried stride of a mountaineer heading home. Cola di Rienzo remains standing alone among the broken columns, his purple cloak flapping uselessly in the cold evening wind. The headless marble statue lies silent in the dust, and the Roman night descends with absolute, unfeeling indifference.)*
