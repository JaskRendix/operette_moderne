# Dialogue of Baldwin and Averroes under the Portico of Córdoba

## Characters

- **Ibn Rushd (Averroes)** — The great commentator of Córdoba, grand cadi and philosopher. He speaks with the rhythmic, luminous precision of a mind that believes the universe is a coherent, eternal machine intelligible through human reason.
- **Baldwin IV** — The Leper King of Jerusalem, worn by physical rot and statecraft. He speaks with the bruised, sensory immediacy of a man whose body and kingdom are crumbling at the exact same speed.

## Setting

*An isolated, moonlit courtyard in the Great Mosque of Córdoba. The sun has long vanished, leaving a sky of deep indigo. The air is cool, scented with citrus blossoms, damp river silt, and dry parchment. Between them stands a **Silent Third Presence**: an ancient marble basin of still water, fed by an iron pipe that drips once every few long moments, casting concentric rings across the moon’s reflection without ever disturbing the quiet depth of the pool.*

---

## **I. The Illusion Introduced**

**Averroes:**

*(Tracing a geometric figure upon a sheet of parchment with a dry reed pen)*

Consider the prime mover, my Lord. The cosmos is not a play written for human tears, but an eternal, uncreated intellect. Aristotle demonstrated that reason is the highest virtue of the soul, the divine spark that links mortal understanding to the celestial spheres. Through the syllogism, we master the passions; through demonstration, we glimpse the immutable cause. Even in a world fragmented by war and religious strife, the intellect remains whole. Is it not a grand comfort to know that above the tumult of kings and empires, the supreme order of the universe stands immaculate, waiting to be comprehended?

*([Moment of False Hope])*

*Baldwin rests his bandaged forearm against the cool marble pillar. For a fleeting instant, as the gentle scent of orange blossoms drifts on the night air and the clean symmetry of the arches frames the stars, his mind feels weightless—he imagines that if he could only grip the pure logic of the cosmos, his physical torment would dissolve into a minor, harmonious detail of a flawless universal theorem.)*

**Baldwin:**

*(His voice rasping, like linen dragged across dry earth)*

It is a silver tower you build out of words, Master Ibn Rushd. But I have sat upon a throne where the cushions were soaked through with the discharge of my own thighs. Tell me—when the nerve in my thumb died last winter, and the flesh turned black before dropping onto the floor during council, which of your master’s syllogisms ought I to have used to bind it back to the bone?

---

## **II. The Dissection of Meaning**

**Averroes:**

The syllogism does not mend the vessel, King Baldwin; it illuminates the mind that inhabits it. The body is matter—*hyle*—subject to generation and corruption, to decay and accident. But the intellect active, the universal mind, is incorruptible. To grieve the rot of the flesh is to mistake the vessel for the water it carries. Suffering is merely the friction of matter resisting form. Reason allows us to rise above *il patimento* of the flesh, to perceive that your kingdom’s peril and your body’s ruin are but natural corruptions in a lower sphere, while truth remains unblemished.

**Baldwin:**

*(He pulls back his glove slightly, revealing linen bandages yellowed at the wrist)*

You speak of matter as though it were a light cloak one casts off at bedtime. But matter is the only language I have ever been allowed to speak. I led five hundred knights through the sands of Montgisard with my right arm strapped to a wooden splint because I could no longer hold a rein. My men did not die for a geometric proof, Ibn Rushd. They died in the heat because they believed their pain had a name in heaven. If your reason only tells me that my rotting face is an expected accident of the lower sphere, then your reason is not false—it is simply helpless. It is an ointment that smells of ink while the flesh rots beneath it.

*([Sensory Indifference Detail])*

*As Baldwin speaks of his agonizing reign, a single drop of water falls from the iron spigot into the marble basin. A small brown leaf, dried and fragile, floats silently on the surface, moving a fraction of an inch across the moon’s reflection before coming to rest again.)*

---

## **III. The Arid Truth**

**Baldwin:**

*(He leans his forehead against the stone, his breath heavy)*

You think you have conquered the darkness because you categorized it in Arabic and Greek. But your philosophy is only *l’inganno*—the most elegant delusion of all. You call nature an intelligible machine, but she is *la natura matrigna*—a blind, deaf engine that grinds down the philosopher and the idiot with the same mindless indifference.

Where is the harmony in a world that grants a child a kingdom only to strip his skin away piece by piece before he reaches twenty? There is no divine geometry here. There is only *la noia*—the terrifying boredom of watching sand run through an hourglass—and the cold reality of decay. Your Aristotle did not cure the world; he merely gave human arrogance a set of rules to write down while waiting for *il nulla*.

**Averroes:**

*(His pen stops. He looks at the King’s shrouded face, his voice unflinching, carrying the iron pride of the scholar)*

And if it is so? If the universe has no concern for your pain, and if the heavens are silent to the cries of Jerusalem and Córdoba alike... would you have us throw away the lamp simply because it does not warm the room?

Reason does not promise salvation, sire. It promises sight.

**Baldwin:**

I say the lamp only reveals how bare the wall is. All your logic, all my statecraft, all the prayers of the priests and the imams are bound together in *l’infelicità comune*. We are creatures who invented reason so we could measure the precise dimensions of our own execution chamber.

---

## **IV. The Final Irony**

**Averroes:**

*(He turns the dry reed pen between his fingers, staring down at the geometric diagrams on his desk)*

Then let it be an execution chamber. But I would still rather know its dimensions than stumble through the dark pretending it is a palace. To comprehend the law that destroys us—that is the only dignity left to man. It is not an illusion, Baldwin. It is the truth.

**Baldwin:**

*(Lowering his eyes to the darkening floor, his voice dropping to a low, fatal whisper)*

And knowing the truth only makes the cell feel smaller.

**Averroes:**

*(He sets his pen down with deliberate care. He does not yield).*

Yet it remains the lamp.

**Baldwin:**

*(Quietly, without looking back)*

And the frost remains outside.

*(The night deepens. Neither man speaks again. Averroes returns his gaze to his parchment, unmoving in his conviction. Baldwin remains motionless against the pillar, shrouded in his linen bandages. Between them, the iron pipe continues its slow, rhythmic drip into the marble basin, measuring out the silent, indifferent seconds of a universe that cares for neither the King's crown nor the Philosopher's lamp.)*
